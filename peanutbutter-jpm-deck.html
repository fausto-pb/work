Slide 01 · The work

Name the transaction, never the category

Every business pays invoices. Every month. Forever.

Open one step back, with something simply true. Then the core line, with the invoice as the subject — not a person. The work is a property of the transaction, not of whoever happens to be doing it.

An invoice arrives. It has to be checked, matched, coded, approved, paid, and recorded.

That’s the work. We do it — not software a business operates, the work itself, done and answered for.

The scope, said in full

  • Vendor invoices, any channel — checked against purchase orders, receiving records, contracts, or that vendor’s history.
  • Vendor identity and payment details — maintained and verified, with a hard gate on any change to where money goes.
  • Recurring bills and subscriptions.
  • Contractor payments, with tax documentation collected for those we onboard.
  • Payment on terms, including early-payment discounts. Cash-priority decisions escalate to the owner.
  • The entry on their books. Close and statements stay with their accountant.

Then the boundary, as a decision rather than a gap: procurement, payroll, tax filing and treasury are deliberately out of scope.

The failure to avoid

In a real meeting, saying “we do financial operations” made a banker hear finance — investments, factoring, treasury. Never open with a category name. Never open with AI. Name the transaction.

Language discipline

A vendor sends an invoice, which is the document. A business owes a payable. Bills are the internal list of what’s due. Never mix them in one sentence. Never say “machine” — AI does the work, say it directly. Never say “small business” or “SMB”: the customer is any business hiring for accounting or outsourcing it.

If they ask

“So you’re a bookkeeping software company?” No. Software is something a business operates. We are the party that does the work and answers for it. Nobody on their side connects anything, configures anything, or reviews a queue.

“Why leave treasury out?” Because treasury is theirs and yours. We hold the payables side. The moment we start moving cash between accounts we are competing with the bank rather than feeding it.

Why it could compound

Verification done once serves every subsequent customer. Every vendor verified for one business is already verified for the next — and every vendor we pay is itself a business with invoices to pay. A network that gets more valuable and cheaper to serve with each customer, available only to whoever does the work rather than sells the tool. An opportunity ahead of us, not a moat we hold today.

If they ask

“What stops Ramp doing this tomorrow?” Nothing technical. Their board is the obstacle. Moving labor onto cost of goods sold to win a market their sales motion doesn’t reach is a decision a software multiple punishes.

“Aren’t you just an outsourced bookkeeper with better tooling?” The unit economics are the difference. A firm adds a person per client. We add compute per transaction, and the cost of that falls.

“What margin do you actually run at?” I don’t know yet, and I won’t draw a curve I can’t defend. We instrument it from transaction one and I’ll show you the real number when there is one.

Slide 05 · What we built

The densest slide. Talk over it longest.

Go deeper here than anywhere else, in business English. No code, no architecture diagrams — but specific enough that a technical person reading over the banker’s shoulder recognises it as real.

75 essential controls, all built and proven by tests that execute rather than by assertion. Verified through adversarial internal audit: roughly 40 attack attempts against the money path, every one refused. Say plainly that there has been no third-party or independent audit. External attestation is the roadmap, not the record — which is why the fourth number on the slide is zero.

The six areas, one line each

  • Identity. Every actor, human or AI, is cryptographically distinguishable and cannot claim to be another. Per-actor credentials, no shared keys, signature-based authentication.
  • Authority. What each actor may decide is enforced outside the AI itself, with hard ceilings. The system cannot argue its way past a limit, because the limit isn’t in the instructions.
  • Communication. Work passes between parts of the system as structured, validated data rather than one AI instructing another. Every exchange becomes record.
  • Traceability. An append-only ledger, cryptographically hash-chained and anchored outside our own control, so an outsider can verify the record rather than take our word for it.
  • Verification. Work is checked by something other than what produced it. The doer never approves itself.
  • Accountability. Every action traces to a responsible human, and there is a kill switch.

The architectural ideas, named plainly

  • The spine. One authoritative record every process writes to. Nothing happens outside it.
  • A closed-loop, queryable company. The company’s own operations are structured data, so it can be asked questions and answer from evidence rather than from someone’s memory. Every human judgment is captured with its reasoning, and that class of exception stops needing a person.
  • Agent-to-agent interoperability. Built on emerging open standards rather than a proprietary island, so what we operate connects to what customers and partners run.

Standards adopted deliberately rather than invented

OAuth 2.1 with token exchange for delegated authority. The current Model Context Protocol authorization profile for tool access. BIP-340 Schnorr signatures for actor identity, implemented and verified against the full official test-vector set and independently cross-checked. Append-only Postgres with cryptographic hash chaining and external anchoring for the record. NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework as the documentation spine, with ISO/IEC 42001 and SOC 2 Type II as the attestation path. EU AI Act Article 50 disclosure applied everywhere by default.

Operating licenses — define the phrase, it means nothing without it

An operating license is an internal permission gating what the system may do at all. Three exist: moving money, acting without human approval, and sending communications unsupervised. Each stays unsigned until its preconditions are met, and the refusal is written into the source code rather than a setting someone can toggle.
